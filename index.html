Norms for success: a starter guide for new GiveWhales

I'm James, GiveWell's incoming chief of staff, and I'm still learning GiveWell's culture. This is a list of practices that help me get the best results from the team, especially around Slack and messaging, that I don't think are necessarily natural or customary for everyone coming to GiveWell.

I thought a reference guide to how I actually work might be useful. A caveat: I don't always live up to these ideals myself, and you'll see me break them. These are my suggestions, not rules, and other GiveWhales may reasonably disagree.

Every norm is numbered, so you can react by number ("yes to 1 and 4, 3 needs a call"). The reason matters more than the rule: if a norm doesn't serve its reason in a given case, use judgment.

Slack and messaging norms

These seven make messages faster to read, easier to answer and harder to lose.

1. Reply in thread wherever you can. Threads keep a channel scannable and keep the context in one place. Post at the top level only when the whole group needs the context, or when the thread has changed subject in a way others would want to see.
2. Put the subject in the first five words. People triage channels from the preview line. Example: "Writing a reply to the reporter's email, and..." or "Sharing John's CE modeling thoughts".
3. For time-sensitive things, state a default action. Silence then moves the work forward instead of stalling it. Example: "James emailed us asking for a reply on scheduling. I plan to respond by Thursday noon ET with option B, unless someone replies otherwise."
4. Use numbered bullets, not round ones. Numbers let people answer precisely. Example replies: "Yes to 1, 2 and 4, but 3 needs a call" or "I follow the others, but what does 3 mean?"
5. If a message will draw replies to several parts, make it a Google Doc. Comments and suggestions then attach to the specific line, not to a long thread. Post the link with a one-line summary and the question you want answered.
6. If something needs a decision, tag one person by name to own it. A name creates a notification and an owner. A general question creates neither. Example: "@James, or someone else if better placed, please reply to this email" and not "Can somebody respond to this email?"
7. Hyperlink every document you mention. Readers should be one click from the source. Example: "this doc says X and this doc says Y", with both phrases linked.

More norms suggestions

These extend the same idea: make it cheap for the other person to read, answer and act.

Asks and decisions

8. Lead with the ask, then give the context. Busy readers answer in slivers of time and may only read the first line. Example: "I plan to do X, though I could do Y. Does X seem right?" followed by background.
9. Make every ask answerable with a yes, a no, a date or a name. Avoid "thoughts?" and "please review". If someone has to read a doc, name the section and the question. Example: "Can you read section 3 and tell me whether Dec 12 works."
10. Give every ask a by-date, and say whether it is flexible. "By Wednesday, flexible to Friday" lets people prioritize honestly.
11. Propose, don't ask permission. "I'll send this at 3 PM ET unless you'd rather send it" is faster for everyone than "Do you want to send it or shall I?"
12. Bring a recommendation, the alternative, and how you could be wrong. A recommendation alone asks the reader to trust you. The alternative and the stakes let them check you quickly.
13. Say what a claim rests on. "The grantee says now is the moment" is different from "now is the moment". "I haven't worked out why yet" beats a plausible guess. This matters more at GiveWell than at most places.
14. Flag a slip the moment you see it coming. Say the date is at risk, give the new date, and say what else moves. A late warning costs more than the slip.

Slack habits

15. Close the loop. React or reply so the sender knows you saw it. Say when it is done. If you will not get to it, say that too.
16. Default to a channel over a DM. Others can learn from the answer, and the next new hire can search for it. Keep DMs for sensitive or personal matters.
17. Write times with a time zone, and add the reader's zone. GiveWell works across Pacific, Eastern and UK/Europe. Example: "10 AM PT / 1 PM ET / 6 PM UK".
18. Don't expect or imply an off-hours reply. Use scheduled send for messages written late. If something is urgent, say so in the first words and say how to reach you.
19. If it is a task, put it in Asana with an owner and a due date. Slack-only requests get lost. Link the Slack thread in the task for context.

Docs

20. Start every doc with the bottom line, the owner, the date and the status. A reader should know in ten seconds what the doc concludes and whether it is still live.
21. Check sharing settings before you link a doc. A link the reader cannot open costs a round trip.
22. Comment with a named person and a specific question, and suggest edits in suggestion mode. Resolve comments once handled so open comments mean open issues.

Meetings

23. Send the agenda and any preread a day ahead, and link them in the invite. If people have read it, skip the voiceover and go straight to discussion.
24. End every meeting with decisions, owners and dates, and post them within a day. Post in the relevant thread or channel so people who were not in the room can see them.

Being new

25. Ask "why?" early and often, in public channels. Your fresh eyes are useful for a short window. Keep a running list of what confused you: it is the best input to the next version of this guide.
26. Learn how your manager likes to work, and tell them how you do. Ask whether they have a "working with me" doc. If not, cover it in an early 1:1: how they like asks, how fast they expect replies, how they give feedback.
27. Share work early and rough. A half-finished outline on day two gets better direction than a polished draft on day ten.
28. Check names, numbers and dates before you send. Small errors in a shared tracker or message spread to everyone who reads it.

Quick facts new hires ask about

  1. Pay dates (US): the 6th and 21st of each month.
  2. Time off: 31 days a year of holidays plus vacation combined, 10 sick days, and the office closes the week of July 4. Record time off before payroll runs, or it has to be fixed by hand.
  3. US holidays (11): New Year's Day, Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents' Day, Memorial Day, Juneteenth, Independence Day, Labor Day, Columbus Day/Indigenous Peoples' Day, Thanksgiving, the Friday after, and Christmas Day. You can work a holiday and take another day instead.
  4. Money you can spend: see the Expense Policy for the wellness stipend, professional development allowance, phone and internet reimbursement, workstation budget and coworking budget.
  5. Working late or early: a cab or rideshare is covered before 7:00 or after 8:00 PM for a work reason. Ask your manager first if it will be more than once a month.
  6. Meals: covered when traveling, with external partners, and at team events on your manager's budget. Dinner when working late is not listed in the policy.
  7. Benefits enrollment (US): open enrollment runs in November for coverage starting December 1, in Employee Navigator.

First 30 days: a short checklist

  • Ask your manager how they like to receive asks, and how fast they expect replies
  • Find out which channels your team uses for what, and where decisions get recorded
  • Set your Slack profile with your time zone and working hours
  • Learn where your team tracks tasks, and put your own there from day one
  • Start a "things that confused me" list and share it with your manager at week four
  • Try norms 1 to 7 on your first ten channel posts
